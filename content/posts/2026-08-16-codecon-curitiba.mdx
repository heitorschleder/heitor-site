A route parameter that accepted `/` was enough to reach every patient record on
the server.

That happened live at CodeCon, in Curitiba, on August 14th and 15th, during a
Capture the Flag run by YuriRDev. The target was an appointment scheduling
system.

## It didn't start with the path traversal

What I didn't expect was how much of the CTF was failing. Before finding
anything, he went through a checklist of the usual suspects, one at a time:

- **Environment variables.** Was the app leaking its env anywhere reachable
  from the outside?
- **Stored credentials.** Were the user's credentials sitting somewhere they
  shouldn't be?
- **Token expiration.** Did the session token actually expire when it was
  supposed to?

None of those gave him anything. He kept going, trial and error, until one did.

That was the best part of watching it live. A hack in a talk slide is a clean
line from vulnerability to exploit. A hack in real time is someone trying the
next thing, and the next, until something gives. It's execution to exhaustion,
not a flash of insight.

## The flaw

The path was simpler than I expected. The path param sanitized neither slashes
nor `../`, so the application started reading whatever the attacker typed as a
directory separator. The intended routing broke, and what was sitting on the
server became browsable — all the records, not one.

What got me wasn't the technique. It was realizing the missing validation is the
same kind I write every day without thinking twice: take the id from the route,
use the id.

## Checking my own routes

So I went back and audited the routes in the product I work on. The way we
build it, a path traversal like that one doesn't reach our data: we validate the
format of every route parameter, so a value carrying `/` or `../` is rejected
before the application does anything with it.

## Meeting my first teacher

The other highlight was Gustavo Guanabara's talk. He was my first programming
teacher. The talk was about his path through tech — not much technical content,
and it didn't need any. Getting to see up close, and actually talk to, someone
who taught me so much when I was starting out was really satisfying.

## Two different disciplines

I've always studied development best practices, the do-it-right side. I never
studied the inverse: how someone breaks that on purpose. Those are two different
disciplines, and it took watching the CTF to see how far apart they are.

Next step is being able to reproduce what he did, not just having watched it.
